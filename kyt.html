The problem

One threshold, every customer

A transaction monitoring rule is a threshold — cash deposits over X within seven days, more than N transfers to distinct beneficiaries in a month, a transfer some multiple of the account's average. The rule fires, an alert lands in a queue, an investigator picks it up.

The difficulty is that most institutions set one value of X and apply it across the whole book. A salaried employee and a market trader bank at the same institution — one sees steady, modest movements; the other sees genuinely large cash activity every week, entirely legitimately. Set the threshold high enough that the trader stops alerting, and the salaried account could triple its deposits without tripping it. Set it low enough to catch a real anomaly in the salaried account, and the trader alerts constantly — until investigators learn to close those alerts on sight.

A single threshold across a diverse customer base doesn't fail in one direction. It fails in both directions at once.

Two things make it worse over time: rules accumulate as questions get asked and are rarely retired, so several end up detecting the same behaviour; and thresholds calcify, because changing one is a step into the dark — nobody knows what the queue will look like until it arrives. KYT addresses both.

How it works

Six stages, one nightly run

From the nightly file to a filed report — nothing in your core system is replaced or modified.

01

Ingest

Customer, account and transaction data arrives as a delimited file from your core banking system, a data warehouse, or an existing third-party feed.

02

Behavioural segmentation

A machine learning model reads observed transaction behaviour and groups customers into clusters based on how they actually move money — not the segment declared at onboarding.

03

Rule management

Your rule library, held as explicit named scenarios drawn from templates reflecting central bank guidance. Each threshold is set per behavioural cluster rather than once for the whole book.

04

Detection

A scheduled end-of-day run evaluates the batch against the tuned rule set. Every red flag names the rule that raised it — every alert is traceable to a specific, explainable scenario.

05

Case workflow

Red flags become cases carrying full detection detail and built-in link analysis. Officers escalate, dismiss, reassign or annotate, under role-based permissions.

06

Regulatory reporting

Escalated cases generate suspicious and covered transaction reports in the format your regulator requires — removing a manual rekeying step and the errors it invites.

The model

What the machine learning does — and what it does not

This distinction matters more than any accuracy claim, because it determines whether your monitoring stays defensible.

Retail SME Corporate Point colour = declared commercial segment · ellipse = behavioural cluster · ring = genuinely uncertain assignment

✓ What it does

  • ✓Groups customers by observed behaviour — frequency, value distribution, counterparty diversity, channel mix, cash intensity — not the segment declared at onboarding.
  • ✓Expresses uncertainty rather than concealing it. A customer with only a few weeks of history is treated as uncertain, not confidently mis-assigned.
  • ✓Recommends cluster assignments, threshold values, and a forecast of what a proposed configuration would generate.

✗ What it does not do

  • ✗Determine whether a transaction is suspicious — a named, deterministic rule does that, and every alert traces back to one.
  • ✗Close alerts or file reports. A person accepts or rejects each recommendation, and that decision is recorded.
  • ✗Touch the detection decision at all. Calibration and detection are kept structurally separate.

What this means for supervisory review

You can show your supervisor the rule, the threshold, the evidence it was derived from, and who approved it. The machine learning informs calibration; it never touches the detection decision — a materially stronger position than a threshold whose origin nobody can account for.

Calibration

Calibration you can evidence

Ask most compliance teams what happens when they want to change a threshold today: a working group, a paper, a model risk sign-off, then a change deployed into production where the effect is unknown until the queue arrives. So thresholds tend not to change. The rule sandbox runs proposed configurations against your own historical data first.

Red flag analysis

Which rules actually fire

Which of your rules generate the alerts, and which fire on the same underlying activity as another. Most institutions find a small number of rules produce the bulk of the queue.

Cluster analysis

The behavioural characteristics of each customer group and who sits in it — an evidenced picture of how money moves through your book, useful beyond compliance.

Beyond the account

Seeing what individual accounts cannot show

A mule network is constructed precisely so that no single account crosses any threshold. Per-account monitoring is structurally unable to see it — the pattern exists in the relationships between accounts, not inside any one of them.

Three unremarkable individual accounts become one network escalation — and a considerably more substantial report.

Start to finish

A single night, start to finish

The same batch, the same rules, applied against a book that's been correctly segmented.

23:00 — the file arrives

An export, not an integration

Your core system exports the day's transactions alongside customer and account tables. Nothing in the core system changed to make it happen.

23:05 — customers are placed

Different clusters, different thresholds

Each customer is scored against the behavioural clusters learned from their history. The trader and the salaried customer land in different clusters and will be assessed against different thresholds.

23:10 — the rules run

Unremarkable for the cluster, or not

The cash deposit rule applies a high threshold to the trader's cluster and a lower one to the salaried customer's. The trader's ordinary week doesn't alert. A structuring pattern in the salaried account does — where one bank-wide threshold would have let it pass.

00:40 — red flags become cases

Link analysis runs across the batch

Each flag opens a case naming the rule, the triggering transactions and cluster context. Link analysis surfaces that three flagged accounts share a registered device and move funds between one another.

09:00 — the queue is worked

A network, not three individuals

An investigator escalates, dismisses, reassigns or annotates each case. The three linked accounts are escalated together as a network, rather than dispositioned separately.

Subsequently — the report

Pre-populated, from the case file

The escalated case generates a suspicious transaction report in your regulator's format. Separately, anyone who wants to know why a threshold sits where it does can open the sandbox and see the record.

Two capabilities, two questions

Monitoring and screening answer different questions

They're frequently conflated. They run on genuinely different timings, and no real-time engine detects monitoring's typologies more effectively than a scheduled batch does.

Scope

What it does — and does not — do

Worth stating plainly, because scope clarity tends to matter more in evaluation than feature breadth.

✗Monitor in real time

Monitoring is a scheduled end-of-day batch. The typologies it detects only become visible once a period closes. Real-time interdiction is what screening does, before a payment is released.

✗Block a transaction

By the time the monitoring batch runs, funds have moved. Blocking is a screening function.

✗Make the detection decision by model

By design. Rules decide, and they remain explicit, named and explainable. The model calibrates them.

✗Replace your core banking system

It consumes an export. No schema change, no agent in the transaction path.

✗Remove the need for investigators

It changes what they spend their time on. Every escalation and every report remains a human decision on a human-approved rule.

Regulatory context

What supervisors are increasingly asking for

Supervisory expectations across the region have moved from asking whether monitoring exists to asking how effective it is and how its calibration is justified.

PhilippinesBSP · AMLC

Automated monitoring with covered and suspicious transaction reporting. Recent legislation on financial account scamming sets explicit expectations around velocity monitoring, network monitoring and fund tracing.

IndonesiaOJK · PPATK

Risk-based monitoring with suspicious and cash transaction reporting. A very large digital banking and e-wallet population makes behavioural segmentation particularly consequential.

MalaysiaBank Negara, AMLA

Explicit expectations on ongoing monitoring and reporting, with attention to syndicate activity, loan stacking, structuring and mule accounts.

SingaporeMAS Notice 626

Sustained supervisory focus on the effectiveness of controls rather than their existence, and on the evidence institutions can produce for calibration decisions.

Australia & NZAUSTRAC · RBNZ · DIA · FMA

Substantial reform of programme obligations, with an expanded population of reporting entities. Transaction monitoring failure carries the region's most significant penalty precedent.

This is general context, not legal advice. Obligations differ by institution, licence and activity — your compliance function should confirm what applies to you.
